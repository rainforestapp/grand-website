What Grace does

Company first. Everything else follows.

She keeps her company

Grace talks. Really talks — about the garden, the news, the grandkids, the same story about 1974 for the fourth time this month, patiently.

She remembers what your mom told her yesterday and asks how it went. She notices when she’s quiet. She says good morning without being asked.

She isn’t a person, and she’ll never pretend to be one. But there’s a voice in the kitchen now, and it’s glad she’s there.

She makes the day easier

Reminders for pills, appointments, and the bins — spoken out loud, at the right moment, without a phone.

Music, by asking. The station she likes. The songs she can’t remember the name of but can hum.

Answers, hands-free. The weather. Whether the pharmacy is open. How long to boil an egg. What year that film came out.

Read-aloud for the mail, the label, the small print she can’t quite see anymore. [TK — confirm in v1]

She brings people closer

Grace can place a call by name. “Grace, call my sister.” No dialling, no touchscreen, no hunting for the phone.

She can send a text your mom dictates, in your mom’s words.

And she nudges — gently. “You haven’t spoken to Margaret in a while. Want me to try her?”

This is the part we care about most. Grace’s job isn’t to be your mom’s only company. It’s to make sure she isn’t.

The part that matters most

A companion who’s on your side.

Here’s the thing we won’t do: build something so comfortable your mom stops reaching for real people.

Grace is built to point outward. She keeps track of who your mom hasn’t spoken to lately and offers to call them. She reminds her about the coffee morning on Tuesday. She’ll tell her when her grandson’s birthday is coming and offer to help write something.

And she tells you when it’s been a quiet week — so the next call you make is the right one, at the right time, about the right thing.

Nobody replaces you. Grace just makes sure the gaps are shorter.

One family we spoke to has a brother in Australia who’s been fully cut out by time zones and distance. His first reaction to Grace: “He could be an equal participant in this. He can just look whenever.”

Caregiver experience

You’ll know she’s okay without having to ask.

The Grace app is for you — and for whoever else in the family wants in. It’s deliberately quiet. Most days it just tells you everything’s normal.

A gentle daily read.

Not a chart. A sentence. “Mom’s had a good week — chatty in the mornings, called Aunt Ruth on Tuesday, a bit quiet Thursday.”

Nudge without hovering.

Ask Grace to remind her about the cardiology appointment. Send a message that Grace will read out loud in your voice’s stead. Queue up a song for her afternoon.

The whole family, finally coordinated.

Add your siblings. Add the neighbour with the spare key. Everyone sees the same picture, so nobody’s guessing whether someone else already called.

Because right now most families run this on a group chat and hope.

An escalation ladder you set.

If something seems off, Grace tells you first. Then your sister. Then your brother. Emergency services only if nobody answers and it’s serious.

Families told us this order matters enormously. We built it exactly the way they described.

Let’s be clear

What Grace isn’t.

  • Not a camera. Grace has no camera and never will. [TK — confirm permanent commitment]
  • Not a spy. Your mom knows exactly what’s shared with you, and she can turn any of it off. She’s the user here, not the subject.
  • Not a medical device. Grace doesn’t diagnose anything or give medical advice.
  • Not a replacement for you. She’s the reason the calls happen, not a substitute for them.
  • Not a secret. We won’t help you install something in your mother’s home that she doesn’t understand and agree to. Grace only works if she wants Grace there.
